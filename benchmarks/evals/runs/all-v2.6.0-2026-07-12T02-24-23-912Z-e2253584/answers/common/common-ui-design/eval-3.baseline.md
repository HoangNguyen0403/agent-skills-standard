If the design feels generic, the core problem is likely lack of visual point of view rather than lack of polish. A white background with purple gradient buttons has become a default pattern, so the interface may look competent but forgettable. To fix that, redesign around a stronger brand system and a clearer product personality.

First, define what the product should feel like. For example: precise, bold, calm, technical, premium, playful, or human. That choice should influence the entire UI, including color, type, spacing, imagery, and motion. Without that foundation, the design will keep drifting back toward common startup trends.

Practical ways to make it feel less generic:

- Replace the default palette with a more intentional system, such as warm neutrals plus a sharp accent, deep dark surfaces with restrained highlights, or editorial-inspired monochrome with one vivid color.
- Use typography as a differentiator. A distinctive headline face paired with a highly readable UI body font can create a stronger identity than gradients alone.
- Reduce reliance on decorative button treatments. Buttons should feel crisp and purposeful; shape, contrast, and spacing matter more than glow effects.
- Introduce more specific visual motifs, such as grid structures, framing devices, data textures, illustration style, or icon language tied to the product’s domain.
- Show real product value through screenshots, workflows, or content examples instead of abstract blobs and floating cards.

You can also improve uniqueness through composition. Many generic designs center everything in repeated rounded cards with identical spacing. Break that pattern with more deliberate asymmetry, stronger section pacing, larger moments of emphasis, or denser editorial blocks where appropriate.

That said, “distinctive” should not become “confusing.” Keep the fundamentals solid:

- Clear hierarchy
- Strong contrast
- Predictable interactions
- Accessible color usage
- Consistent component behavior

The goal is a design that is recognizable after one glance. Instead of asking “How do we make this look more modern?” ask “What would make this unmistakably ours?” That shift usually leads to better decisions than adding more gradients or trendy effects.
